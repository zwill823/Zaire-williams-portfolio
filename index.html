MIT Accelerated Materials Laboratory for Sustainability · April 2025 – present

The Furnace

A radiant annealing furnace and its control hardware, built for our system. Discovery, Synthesis, Characterization, and Optimization or DiSCO is an autonomous, ML-guided platform that screens new perovskite solar materials. I inherited a concept a previous student had scoped but never built, and was the sole engineer for the mechanical design, build, and integration. It runs the campaigns today, and I am now instrumenting a robust DAQ system to prove how well it holds temperature uniformly.

Thermal designPID + SSR controlMulti-supply powerThermocouple DAQLow-pressure environmentCAD + 3D printing

Where the furnace fits

One step in a loop that runs by itself.

DiSCO searches for better perovskite solar absorbers without a person driving it. A dispenser lays down a new composition, my furnace crystallizes it, instruments measure photoconductivity, band gap and stability, and a machine-learning model chooses what to make next. The loop repeats as a campaign.

The perovskite is the absorber layer; it's the film that actually catches light and turns it into current. Better compositions mean more efficient, cheaper cells, and when stacked on silicon film as a tandem, they reach record efficiencies. Screening those compositions by hand takes months. The platform does it in days.

Annealing is the step everything downstream inherits. If the heat is uneven across a slide, the samples crystallize differently, and every measurement after that carries the error into the model. The furnace does not have to be exotic. It has to be repeatable.

How it was built

From a folder of notes to a furnace in the system.

  • April – May 2025

    Insulation, heating elements, and a bonding process that had to be invented

    I mapped and dug out the thermocouple holes in the rigid insulation, epoxied terminal blocks, and threaded nichrome wire through each piece. Getting the aluminum face onto the insulation without shorting the heating wire took several attempts.

  • May – June 2025

    One closed loop per zone

    Each zone gets a type-K thermocouple into a PID, the PID driving a solid-state relay, the relay switching a 0–160 V supply into its heating element. I originally prototyped the logic with an LED as the load so I could see the relay latch, then swapped the LED for the actual nichrome.

  • June – July 2025

    Home for the hardware

    I designed 3D-printed shelves for the PIDs and the relay + fan stacks and built an 80/20 polycarbonate enclosure around them. The shelves took a lot of iterations. A bay sized to a PID was a fit but to avoid it overheating I opened it slightly and added vents. A full-height print did not fit the printer bed, so I shortened the spacers and reprinted. The relay fan pocket was too small three separate times which taught me the importance of tolerances.

  • 18 – 19 July 2025

    Installation + debugging days

    The thoughtful structural designs made installation clear and straight foward. Once the furnace was installed, several PIDs updated slowly or not at all. A baseline test measuring voltage and current on every channel sorted the zones: high voltage with no current meant an open heating element, normal supply with no response at the load meant a dead relay drive. The open elements were in the furnace and unreachable in place. The relay faults were in my enclosure, so I pulled it, replaced every heat-shrink splice with crimp terminals, and built an LED tester to watch a PID call for heat without waiting on a thermal response.

  • October 2025

    Kapton curtains

    The chamber runs at low pressure, so there is almost no convection. So in our environment heat only conducts and radiates, and the open sides bleed it out. I fitted overlapping Kapton curtains with weighted hems on all four sides, sized to seal against the shell without fouling the moving transporter. The chamber went from stalling near 120 °C to holding 200 °C. Ramp rate was still slower than we wanted, which set up the next year of work.

  • December 2025 – February 2026

    Transporter = heat sink?

    Slides were annealing unevenly at the edges. The aluminum glass-slide transporter was acting as a heat sink, wicking heat out of the slide edges. The fix was heating the transporter but a moving carriage needs continuous power without tangling or snapping a wire. I reverse-engineered a spring-return clothesline for its flat spiral metal spring and designed a spool assembly around it, plus a guide wheel to steer the wire to and from the carriage. I proved the final version on a wooden block before it went into DiSCO.

  • 2026

    Constant rebuilds, and the version running now

    We kept changing the architecture for optimization: heated wire in linear runs instead of coils and a furnace that slides out of the DiSCO setup for service. I built a sawing rig to cut the rigid insulation grooves for the wire housing and then drew and cut the new shells. Aleks, my advisor, carried it further over the summer. The fine 0.2 mm Nickrothal wire was snapping at the hot edges, so it was replaced with 1 mm Kanthal APM, which has held. The wiring was reworked and the control hardware grew to ten PID loops.

What the measurements changed

Two problems, and the rebuild they justified

Through 2025, I logged furnace data far more than these two figures show. But almost all of it pointed at the same pair of faults; it took too long to get to temperature, and it did not get there evenly. These are the two measurements that settled the argument for tearing the element layout apart.

2025 configuration

It took ten minutes to get where a hot plate gets in one

A single thermocouple on a glass slide at the edge position; the worst case, farthest from the most active zones and nearest an exit path.

A hot plate in open air is at a stable temperature almost immediately. Inside the chamber there is no convection or conduction to help, and the thermocouple needed the full ten minutes to reach 170–175 °C. Every slide has to see the same thermal history, so the slowest position sets the cycle which means this curve, not the average, is the one that matters. The Kapton curtains had already taken steady-state from stalling near 120 °C up to 200 °C. But even with this fix, there was still heat leaking at the edges that needed to be taken care of.

2025 configuration

One zone was not being driven

Steady-state zone readings at a 250 °C set-point. Five zones hold; outer bottom sits roughly 85 °C low, carried mostly by heat bleeding in from its neighbors.

Why one dead zone was the bigger finding

Every zone has had its own thermocouple, PID and relay since the first build, so this was never a case of missing control. The zone was being commanded correctly and simply could not answer. Its heating elements had failed, and the reading you see is mostly heat bleeding in from the neighbors.

That is the part that we sat with. The PID sees a zone running cold and does the only thing it can, which is drive the remainder harder. This causes faster wear, skewing of the field, and reporting nothing wrong. The fault is invisible until someone measures it by hand.

These issues made us decide that the element layout itself was the thing to change, rather than anything in the control circuit.

The rebuild

Fewer elements, finer control

The layout went through three generations. The last one is the point: ten zones, ten elements, one PID each so a zone and an element are now the same thing.

Build one · 2025

Annular wiring

  • Elements 6
  • Zones 6
  • Elements per zone 1

Three concentric rectangular runs per plate: inner, middle, outer. It never reached the temperatures we needed and could not hold them evenly across a slide. Some zone's elements had also failed, so they could not be driven at all.

Rebuild · linear

Linear wiring

  • Elements 40
  • Zones 10
  • Elements per zone 4

Straight runs spread heat far better and finer zoning gave more control. But four elements share one loop, so a failure is invisible and driving the fine wire hard enough for real uniformity kept snapping it.

Running now

One element, one loop

  • Elements 10
  • Zones 10
  • Elements per zone 1

Ten runs bent to shape, five above and five below, each on its own PID. Thicker wire that stops breaking, a quarter of the parts, and the best control and uniformity yet

Top plate shown; the bottom mirrors it. Colour marks the control zone each element belongs to.

Why I trust it now

Around the final wiring rebuild, the rest of the work compounds. The Kapton curtains hold the heat in. The retractable spool keeps the transporter powered through its full travel and heated when necessary, so the slide edges no longer deal with a sink. The 1 mm Kanthal APM wires have held sturdy, which removes the snapping failure that was ending tests. The furnace slides out of the machine, so a bad element is a service job rather than a teardown. And DiSCO's newest monitoring stack with seven camera livestreams, automated calibration, and a Slack bot that raises faults on its own, means nobody has to be hovering over each shift.

Campaigns now run through without intervention, which they did not reliably do a year ago.

What I cannot claim yet: that the ten-zone build is uniform. I know campaigns run and samples come out right but this furnace has not been characterized to the standard the old one was. There is no measured thermal map of it, and no numbers for proper documentation. That is what the instrumentation below is for.

Where the confidence comes from

  • Failures are isolated. One element per zone means a fault lands in one place instead of being absorbed by neighbors.
  • Failures are visible. A zone that stops responding shows up in the data, rather than hiding behind a PID driving harder.
  • Failures are rarer. The thicker Kanthal APM elements have held where the fine Nickrothal kept snapping.
  • Failures are cheap to fix. The furnace slides out because of modular reconstruction so elements are easily individually replaceable.
  • Nobody has to be watching. Camera monitoring and automated fault alerts catch what a person standing there used to.

What I'm working on now — Fall 2026

Making the temperature data defensible.

My current job is to measure the furnace well enough that the numbers stand on their own.

The instrument question

Why not just keep the Arduino?

My measurement system last semester was on an Arduino Uno with eight to ten MAX31856 amplifiers on a shared SPI bus. It got us started but the problem was the cold junction compensation: the MAX compensates internally, but only if the terminals sit at a known, stable temperature. On a breadboard or a hand-soldered perfboard there is no controlled isothermal region, so the error is not a fixed offset.

What I looked for

Eight to ten channels, and mini sockets

Our probes already terminate in yellow mini-K plugs but most of them jump well past budget and ship with GUI-only software, which rules out streaming into the platform or local distribution. The Pico TC-08 was the only candidate left: eight channels plus a cold-junction channel, a free logging application, and a documented SDK with Python examples.

Calibration

Two considerations

Absolute accuracy is what lets the paper say the furnace was at a given temperature and defend it against perovskite literature. Channel-to-channel consistency is what the uniformity map rests on, whether a few degrees between two probes is a real gradient or two channels disagreeing.

Giving the furnace a voice in the monitoring stack

Thermocouple data should stream into the monitoring stack the lab just built, timestamped and tagged with a campaign ID, so a sample's thermal history stays attached to the sample. It is also what unattended operation requires: a zone going cold has to flag itself.

Characterization, then the paper

Spatial uniformity across the annealing zone, top and bottom, and drift over repeated multi-hour anneals. The paper will document the entire architecture, the heated-transporter interface and the validated performance. The open-source release turns the rig into something another lab can rebuild.

Illustrative — not measured data

What the characterization should produce

Eight channels. The target is every zone inside a ±10 °C band at steady state, and arriving there together. This is a model of the expected output, drawn to define what "uniform enough" means.

MIT 16.831 Space Systems Engineering · Spring 2026

ASTEROIDS CubeSat

Asteroid Subsurface Terrain Exploration Radar for Orbit Ice Detection Survey. A 2U CubeSat analog built to demonstrate detection of asteroid subsurface ice, flown on a zipline over three test days. I was electronics and integration lead working on the sensor suite, the wiring and harness, and getting three people's subsystems to work as one.

2U CubeSatAdafruit Feather RP2040Thermal sensorsRadio communicationData utilizationConcept to creation

The pivot

We changed the detection method days before flying.

The design started around radar-style detection using an ultrasonic sensor. Building it, I found the ultrasonic part did not support I²C while the thermal sensor did. That one integration fact changed the architecture.

I restructured the system around an Adafruit Feather as the central microcontroller rather than routing communication through the Raspberry Pi. The thermal sensor went on over a STEMMA I²C connection while the ultrasonic sensor needed header pins soldered on to interface at all. Making the Feather the controller simplified integration and the whole system got better for it. By the end of the first flight day we were transmitting both distance and temperature from the payload to the ground station, with the system staying powered and stable across runs.

Thermal turned out to be the right physics anyway. Ice shows up as a temperature drop, which is a cleaner signal to defend.

Debugging, then an analysis plan

The second flight day opened with battery problems and inconsistent I²C communication, enough to stop stable operation. We worked through them systematically, restored full function, and reproduced the first day's results which is the part that matters, because a result you cannot repeat is not reliable data.

With the pipeline reliable, I moved to how to actually present the data: distance vs. time, temperature vs. time, and a temperature map to show spatial variation across the thermal sensor's field. Then we validated the whole approach the simplest way available, quickly with an ice cube placed near the sensors. It worked, cleanly and consistently, and gave us the controlled baseline for the real demonstration.

Flight day, and a failure that didn't matter

On the final day we set up early to check every connection before the graded run. Our remote access method was TigerVNC, which had been fine indoors but became unstable outside so we could not reliably reach the payload and ground station from our laptops.

Rather than burn the window on it, we connected directly to the ground station by IP on a monitor and carried on; the data kept coming without the remote layer. The demonstration went through successfully: distinct temperature drops in the plots showing ice detection, distance data consistent over time, and sensor readings synchronized well enough to present a coherent analysis. I also ran onboard SD card logging as redundancy, so a dropped link would not cost us the dataset.

What I owned

  • Sensor suite design and assembly: thermal and ultrasonic sensors, RP2040 microcontroller, 915 MHz LoRa radio.
  • Wiring, PCB layout and harness integration inside a 2U volume with hard mass and power budgets.
  • Co-authoring the mission proposal, including CONOPS diagrams in TikZ and an RF link-budget analysis.
  • Integrating the flight chassis and presenting results to faculty and peers.

The lesson I actually took away was about sequencing. Integration is deciding what has to work first so that everyone else can build on top of it, and being willing to throw out an architecture when a datasheet tells you a certain truth.

GE Vernova · Gas Power · Summer 2026 · Greenville, SC

Aeroderivative repair and industrialization

My team turned probable fixes into a repair that can be produced repeatably and at volume. I worked five parallel workstreams across characterization, materials evaluation, tooling and industrialization with one question underneath all of them: can we measure this part well enough to understand it and construct a repair?

Eddy-current NDEBlue-light 3D scanningMetallographySEM / EDSDigital microscopySiemens NX

This page describes method and approach only. Specific measurements, part identifiers and results stay inside GE Vernova.

The gap that creates the work

A one-off repair is an engineering test. A producible one is an engineering feat.

It needs characterized geometry and damage, qualified materials, a documented process and an approved supply chain. Most of my summer was spent on the first part.

Workstream one

Reverse engineering a hot-section nozzle

Two investigations on two different parts, designed to meet in the middle. One track worked forward from an existing model toward a better-defined internal definition in Siemens NX. It was built as modular castable segments so one region can be adapted without rebuilding the whole model. The other track worked backward from 2 identical pieces of field-return hardware: non-destructive inspection on one part, destructive cut-up on the other.

Where they meet is a characterized part that a repair can be designed. The recurring theme across conversations with other teams was that nobody had a full picture of the part and that gap is why the work exists.

Main Method

A zoning scheme for eddy current

A nozzle is not uniform, and where coating is lost matters more than how much. So rather than take scattered readings, I divided both airfoils and both bands into named zones and measured every part in the same places, averaging repeat readings at each one.

Eddy current works by driving AC through a coil to induce currents in the conductive substrate; because the coating is non-conductive, the impedance shift calibrates to coating thickness. The part survives this process, so it can be measured, scanned, stripped, then measured again.

The result I am proudest of is a limitation

Because a second part went through destructive cut-up, I could compare my non-destructive readings against cross-section measurements at matched locations. This was a direct check on whether the fast method agrees with the slow one.

It agreed well on flat regions and disagreed on tightly curved ones. The reason is in the calibration: it assumes the probe sits flat against the surface. At a tight leading-edge radius it cannot and the impedance shift reads as coating that is not there.

The method supports setting tolerances by region but does not support a point-wise thickness on a curved surface. I flagged it to my assignment leader and recommended treating curved-surface readings as regional indicators until a curvature correction exists.

Workstream two

Fretting wear, and which coating survives the contact

Aeroderivative hardware has loaded joints that micro-slip. A blade-root dovetail oscillates microscopically under load, and that micro-motion is fretting. It matters because fretting fatigue is a crack-initiation mechanism, not just surface cosmetics.

There are three regimes as slip amplitude grows: stick, mixed stick–slip, and gross slip, and the worst place for fatigue life is the middle one, where cracks initiate. The material couple matters too: identical alloys with clean surfaces cold-weld, causing material transfer. A dissimilar counterface resists that but partitions damage differently.

Dry-film lubricants are the countermeasure. Which coating and at what temperature are questions answered with coupon testing.

Choosing comparisons out of a matrix

I did not set the test matrix, so I went looking for the pairs where exactly one variable moved: one comparison isolating the material couple and one isolating temperature, sharing a common baseline.

Turning coupons into numbers

  • Clean and macro-image every wear surface on a copy stand, with and without a scale bar.
  • High-magnification imaging on a Keyence digital microscope; low magnification across the scar, higher magnification on regions of interest, stitched imaging over the full contact patch.
  • Extract parallel profile traces across each scar, exported as CSV; dozens of traces across a dozen surfaces.

I also wrote up open questions inspired by my work: whether coupon measurements meant anything without a pre-test baseline scan, whether a single specimen is enough to decide or whether the fixture is the variable, and whether maximum depth is even the right metric when mean depth or scar area might say something different.

Workstream three

A tool, because the bottleneck moved to analysis

Microscope profile traces were being opened one at a time, plotted by hand, and measured off the screen. That is slow, inconsistent between operators, and does not scale across many traces on many coupons.

The constraint shaped everything: the data is export-controlled, so cloud services, servers and most third-party software are ruled out. The requirement was to make analysis faster without the data ever leaving the machine.

I built a single HTML file with zero dependencies. No install, no admin rights, no IT ticket, no server. It runs fully offline, reads the microscope's CSV export directly, and takes units from the file. Filenames set the group and the curve colour, so it fits the workflow that already existed instead of demanding a new one.

The settings that mattered

  • Baseline correction by linear fit through the unworn ends of a line, which corrects specimen tilt as well as level and it was adjustable per group.
  • An adaptive noise threshold measured from the reference region since finish varies between specimens.
  • Warnings when sampling pitch differs within a group, so incomparable traces do not get quietly compared.

I wrote a user guide documenting the file format, the naming rules, every baseline mode and when to use each, how scar extent is defined, and what each measurement column means. It also closed the loop on my own analysis because the tool allowed my comparisons to sit on a common, comparable scale.

Workstreams four and five

Characterization support, and the pipeline

Coating erosion study

A null result that was still a result

I characterized coating loss and microstructure on a set of eroded thermal-barrier-coating buttons. I worked on cold mounting before and after sectioning then polishing through progressively finer stages.

The outcome was not a coating ranking. Erosion across the set was too shallow to separate the candidates. Reporting a ranking built on differences too small to be real would have been worse than reporting nothing. The finding about the test parameters is what saves the next campaign.

Dimensional characterization

Shroud sectioning and scanning

Dimensional work on a shroud, sectioning perpendicular to the cooling-hole vector to answer specific questions: are the holes conical or is the variation just scatter, what does the recast layer look like around the hole perimeter, and a dimension that was simply missing from the drawings.

3D scanning

Rotor brakes, and a mistake worth keeping

Manual handheld scanning to capture post-failure geometry: magnetic marker balls placed for coverage, meshing in three progressively finer passes, then alignment and post-processing to a semi-watertight model.

Industrialization: a repair is not real until it is producible

A developed repair only creates value once it is released, quoted, sourced, qualified and available. I worked a spare-parts backlog through that pipeline: seven steps per part, five milestone dates in order, export-control compliance handled throughout. Without someone working the list, parts stall silently.

Going part by part surfaced something the per-part view hides: a large share of parts were missing downstream milestone dates entirely, and a substantial number carried dates that disagreed between two systems of record. Status you cannot trust is worse than no status, so surfacing those conflicts is what made my tracking useful for prioritization instead of just reporting.

What the summer taught me

  • Characterization is the start. You cannot design a repair for an un-measured part, and how you measure is itself a design decision.
  • Know where a method stops working. Finding the boundary made the data easier to use, not less valuable.
  • Say what your data does not support. Better to present four open questions than a conclusion data cannot prove.
  • A null result is a result, if you can say why.
  • The work you find is as real as the work you are given. Three of my assignments, and the tool I am proudest of, started with a message to someone I had never met.
  • Industrialization is where engineering meets reality. Repeatable, compliant and producible, or it does not move forward.

Autonomous soft drone

Hands-on work on a compliant "soft" drone built to track and manipulate both stationary and moving objects.

ROS 2NVIDIA JetsonPixhawkPose trackingGrasp planningTrajectory optimization

What I did

Working at the seam between hardware and software.

On the bench: Jetson compute, Pixhawk flight control, and the sensor and power wiring between them. In the stack: integrating and upgrading the ROS 2 framework to improve pose tracking, grasp planning and trajectory optimization.

A soft airframe changes the problem. Compliance that makes grasping safer also makes the vehicle harder to model, so pose estimation and trajectory planning have to tolerate a body that does not hold its shape the way a rigid frame does.

It is the same discipline as the furnace in different clothing. Both real physical systems, closed loops around them, and gaps between what simulation says and what happens when the hardware moves.

SISSA — International School for Advanced Studies · Trieste, Italy · Summer 2024

Neural-network aerodynamic shape optimization

Two and a half months of research through MIT MISTI, writing and deploying neural-network simulations that dynamically optimize a wing's shape and the aerodynamic domain around it.

Neural networksPhysics-informed networksMATLABSignal & image processingTime-series analysis

The work

Learning the computational side of a problem I would later meet in hardware.

Conventional aerodynamic shape optimization is expensive because every candidate geometry needs its own flow solution. The approach here was to train networks that learn the mapping including physics-informed networks so the model is constrained by fluid physics rather than fitting data blindly.

I coded and deployed the simulations: multilayer-perceptron and physics-informed architectures that adjust an airfoil's shape and the surrounding domain toward better performance. Then I used MATLAB for the statistical analysis, signal and image processing, and time-series analysis that turned the raw simulation output into something you could draw a conclusion from.

What I actually learned

The technical lesson was about learning how to trust the model. A network will happily produce a smooth, plausible-looking optimized shape and the only defense is checking output against physics, which is why half of the work was analysis.

The other half of the experience was not technical. I was nineteen, in a country I had never been to, in a research group whose first language was not my own, on a problem I had not studied yet. Figuring out how to be useful in that situation like asking for context early, reading enough to ask better questions, accepting that the first two weeks would be slow, are skills I have used in every lab since.

It also set up everything after. I met this aerodynamics computationally before I met it in coursework, and later in a compressor and a turbine at GE Vernova. Coming at the same physics from the analytical side first made the hardware side easier to understand in industry.
